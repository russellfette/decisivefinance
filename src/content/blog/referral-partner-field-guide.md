## At a glance

- If you advise owners, a client with un-provable numbers is your risk too. A re-trade or a collapse in diligence costs you the fee, the timeline, and the client's confidence.
- The signals that predict it show up long before the process starts, and they're readable from the outside if you know what to look for.
- Getting ahead of it protects the deal and the relationship, because the fix has to happen before the numbers age into the buyer's trailing period.

This one is for the advisors, brokers, bankers, and attorneys who send owners into sale processes. A client whose numbers won't survive diligence is a problem you inherit, and the earlier you spot it, the more of the deal you can protect. Here are the signals, and when to act on them.

## Why is the client's numbers problem your problem?

Because you're judged on the outcome, and the outcome often turns on numbers you didn't prepare. When a deal re-trades or dies in diligence over a believability problem, the cost lands on you as much as the owner: the fee evaporates, the timeline blows up, and the client remembers that the process you ran fell apart. The trigger usually traces back to numbers the owner could never quite stand behind, which was knowable before the process began.

So spotting the problem early isn't a favor to the client. It's how you protect your own deal, your fee, and the relationship that sends you the next one.

## What are the signals a client will re-trade?

They're readable without opening the books, if you know the tells:

1. **The owner hedges when asked about margins.** "It's around" and "roughly" where you'd expect precision means the numbers aren't understood, and a buyer will find the same fog.
2. **The close is slow.** Books that land a month or more after period-end signal an uncontrolled finance function, which a buyer reads as risk.
3. **One customer dominates the story.** A client who talks constantly about one big account is telling you where the concentration discount lives.
4. **Everything runs through the owner.** If you can't get an answer from anyone but the principal, neither can a buyer, and the owner-dependence discount is coming.
5. **The add-back wish list is long.** An owner already rehearsing an ambitious list of adjustments is setting up the schedule a buyer will strike down.
6. **The owner won't show you the numbers.** The most reliable signal of all. An owner who deflects from his own P&L knows, at some level, that it wouldn't survive a stranger.

Any two of these together, and the client is likely sitting on a diligence problem that will surface at the worst time unless it's addressed first.

## When should you get ahead of it?

As early as you can, because the fix is trailing-period work. Corrections made in the quarter the process starts document that the problems were recent; corrections made a year ahead are simply how the business runs by the time a buyer looks. The advisors who protect their deals raise the numbers question early, while there's still time for the fix to age into the track record a buyer will pay for.
