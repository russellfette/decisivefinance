## At a glance

- A building the owner rents to his own company at $60,000 a year against $180,000 of market rent inflates earnings by $120,000. A buyer normalizes it back, and at a 5x multiple that is $600,000 of value removed on one lease.

A related-party line is worth whatever you can document, and on a typical owner-led company the gap between documented and undocumented is about $255,000 of earnings, or roughly $1,275,000 of price at a 5x multiple. That is not a penalty for doing anything wrong. It is what happens when a buyer meets a set of transactions between the company and its owner and has no independent way to price any of them.

Every owner-led company has these lines. The building held in a separate LLC. The spouse on payroll. The affiliate that buys product at cost. The truck, the phone, the trip that was mostly business. None of it is unusual, and almost none of it is documented the way a buyer needs it documented. In diligence, that turns a normal set of facts into a set of open questions, and open questions get priced conservatively.

## What does a buyer actually do with a related-party line?

He removes it and replaces it with what an unrelated third party would have charged. That is the whole exercise. He is not trying to catch anyone. He is trying to answer one question: what would this company earn if it were owned by someone else and had to pay market rates for everything it consumes.

So the below-market lease comes out and market rent goes in. The owner's compensation comes out and a hired general manager's salary goes in. The affiliate's cost-plus-nothing pricing comes out and a market price goes in. What survives is the earnings a stranger would inherit, and that is the number the multiple gets applied to.

The direction matters more than owners expect. Below-market rent flatters the P&L, so correcting it costs the seller. Above-market rent understates earnings, so correcting it should pay the seller, and it only does if the correction is documented well enough that the buyer will underwrite it. Unsupported corrections in the seller's favor get struck. Corrections in the buyer's favor never need support, because the burden sits on the party asking for the adjustment. That asymmetry is why the same set of facts can move $255,000 in either direction depending on who prepared for the conversation.

## Which lines get tested every single time?

There are five, and a diligence team works through them in roughly this order:

1. **Real estate and rent.** If the operating company leases from an entity the owner controls, the buyer replaces the rent with a market rate and often asks for a broker opinion or a comparable lease. A company paying $60,000 on a building that would rent for $180,000 loses $120,000 of earnings in the normalization, which is $600,000 of value at 5x. It matters just as much in reverse, and a lease at $240,000 when the market is $180,000 is $60,000 of earnings the seller can recover, if the market rate is supported.
2. **Family and non-working payroll.** A relative on the books at $95,000 who does not hold a role the company needs is a legitimate add-back, and it is the single most commonly rejected one. The buyer wants to see that the cost actually goes away at closing. A name on a payroll register is not evidence of that. A separation letter, a job description that no longer exists, and an org chart that closes without the seat are.
3. **Affiliate purchases and sales.** When a sister company buys at cost or sells at a discount, both sides of the invoice are set by the same person. The buyer restates both to market and, because he cannot verify the volume would survive a change in ownership, sometimes discounts the revenue entirely. This is the line most likely to move the multiple rather than just the earnings.
4. **Shareholder loans and receivables.** Money moving between the owner and the company without a note, a rate, or a repayment history is a balance-sheet question that becomes a working-capital question at closing. Undocumented, it usually gets treated as debt-like and settled out of the purchase price.
5. **Personal expenses run through the company.** The vehicle, the travel, the meals, the membership. Roughly $40,000 on a company this size, and every dollar of it is creditable with a receipt and a business purpose, and zero of it is creditable without. This is where owners lose the most money for the least reason.

## Why do these lines cost more than they should?

Because they arrive as a surprise, in a window the seller does not control, at the point in a process where the seller has the least room to push back. An owner who hears about the rent normalization for the first time in week six of exclusivity is not negotiating. He is absorbing.

And these lines do a second kind of damage. A buyer who finds three undocumented related-party items stops treating the rest of the financials as independently reliable. The specific dollars come off the earnings, and then the confidence discount comes off the multiple. That compounding is the same pattern behind [the add-backs a buyer will believe versus the ones he'll strike](/perspectives/add-backs-believe-vs-strike) and behind why [owners fail a Quality of Earnings on their own numbers](/perspectives/quality-of-earnings-owners-fail). One weak line is a correction. Three weak lines is a character reference.

There is also a timing problem. Fixing a lease to market today does not clean up the trailing twelve months a buyer will read. It starts a clock. The corrected rent has to age into the track record before it counts, which means the useful window for this work is measured in quarters ahead of a sale, not weeks.

## What does documented actually look like?

For each related-party line, three things: the agreement in writing, the market benchmark that shows the rate is arm's length, and the evidence of what happens to the item at closing. That is it. A lease, a broker letter, and a statement that the lease continues at that rate. A payroll record, a job description, and a plan showing the seat is gone. An intercompany invoice, a third-party price list, and a supply agreement.

Most owners already have two of the three on most lines. The one that is almost always missing is the market benchmark, because nobody needed it while the owner sat on both sides of the transaction. It is also the cheapest of the three to go get, and it is worth several hundred thousand dollars of price on the rent line alone.

Assembling this is not an accounting exercise, and it is not something a monthly close produces on its own. It is a specific file, built to answer a specific stranger's question. That is the same discipline behind [accurate and ugly beating polished and untrustworthy](/perspectives/accurate-and-ugly-beats-polished) and behind [the owner-dependence discount](/perspectives/owner-dependence-discount): what a buyer can verify is what a buyer will pay for.
