## At a glance

- A letter of intent hands the calendar to the buyer. Exclusivity typically runs 60 to 90 days, and every week you spend assembling data he asked for is a week off a clock you cannot pause.
- Extensions are not free. A buyer who grants one usually prices it. On a $24M enterprise value, a 5% concession to keep the deal alive is $1,200,000, and it is paid by the seller who could not answer on schedule.
- The answer speed is set before the LOI, not after. We cut a monthly close from 22 days to 3 and build the answer file a diligence team will ask for, in 14 days. One guarantee: at least 3x the fee in owner-accepted value, in 14 days, or you pay nothing.

The day you sign a letter of intent, you give away the calendar, and on a $24M deal the price of getting it back is about $1,200,000. That is the arithmetic of a 5% concession, which is roughly what a buyer asks for in exchange for a 30-day extension when a seller runs out of exclusivity with the schedules still unfinished. Nothing about the business changed in those 30 days. What changed is who was waiting on whom.

Owners spend a lot of energy on the clock they control, the one that runs from the decision to sell to the day they go to market. That clock is real, and [it does not start until the numbers are true](/perspectives/when-the-clock-actually-starts). But there is a second clock, it starts the moment the LOI is signed, and the buyer holds the stopwatch.

## What is exclusivity actually buying?

A letter of intent is not a sale. It is a period, usually 60 to 90 days, during which the seller agrees to stop talking to anyone else while the buyer completes diligence, arranges financing, and negotiates the purchase agreement. The seller gets a price on paper. The buyer gets the only thing he really wanted, which is the right to take his time without competition.

That trade is fine when the seller can keep pace. It goes wrong when he cannot, because the two things the seller gave up are the two things that made the price hold. He gave up the alternative, so there is no second bidder to walk toward. And he gave up the deadline, because once exclusivity is the constraint, the seller needs the deal to close far more than the buyer needs to close it.

Diligence teams work fast, and their requests arrive in waves rather than all at once. The first list lands within days of signing. The follow-ups come off the answers, which means the second list is written after the first one is satisfied, and the third after the second. A seller who takes two weeks to answer each wave has spent six weeks of a ten-week window on three exchanges, and the purchase agreement has not been drafted yet.

## Where does the time actually go?

Not where owners expect. It rarely goes to the hard questions. It goes to the routine ones that nobody in the company can answer quickly because the answer was never assembled in one place.

1. **The monthly close.** A 22-day close means the most recent month is unavailable for three weeks of every four. Under exclusivity, a buyer asking for stub-period results waits on a process that was never built for an audience. A 3-day close makes the same request a same-week answer, which is why [close speed reads as control](/perspectives/close-speed-signals-control) before anyone examines a single number.
2. **Revenue by customer, by month, on a consistent basis.** Most companies can produce revenue by customer, and most can produce it by month, and very few can produce the two together on the same cut across 36 months without someone rebuilding it in a spreadsheet.
3. **The add-back support.** Every normalization on the schedule needs a document behind it. Assembling that evidence during exclusivity, item by item, is how a defensible add-back becomes a withdrawn one, because the seller runs out of window before he runs out of proof.
4. **Contracts and the customer file.** Signed agreements, renewal terms, change orders, and assignment clauses, matched to the customers they belong to. The gap between what the company believes its contracts say and what the executed copies say is discovered here, on the buyer's clock.
5. **The working capital detail.** Monthly balances by component for 12 to 24 months, because the peg is set from history. A seller who supplies this late is negotiating a peg with a buyer who has already modeled one.

None of these are hard questions. They are unassembled ones, and unassembled is expensive only under a deadline.

## What happens when the window closes?

Three things, none of them good for the seller.

The buyer extends and charges for it. This is the common outcome and the cleanest, which is what makes it dangerous. An extension is a moment when the price is open again, and the seller has already told his employees, his banker, and possibly his family that the company is sold. A 5% price concession on a $24M enterprise value is $1,200,000, and it is agreed to in an afternoon by a seller who has spent 90 days and six figures of professional fees getting to that afternoon.

The buyer lets it lapse and keeps working without exclusivity. The seller is technically free to talk to others, and practically he is not, because he has no live alternatives and re-warming the second bidder means explaining why the first one is still in diligence.

Or the deal breaks. The seller returns to a market that now knows the company went under LOI and came back. Some of the buyers who passed the first time will assume diligence found something, and the seller cannot prove otherwise without disclosing the diligence. Meanwhile the trailing twelve has rolled forward by two quarters, and the months that carried the story have aged out. This is the compounding version of [the five findings that re-trade a deal](/perspectives/five-findings-that-retrade-a-deal): the finding costs a number, the delay costs the trend.

## Can the seller take the clock back?

Not after signing. Before signing, almost entirely, and the work is unglamorous.

The lever is answer speed, and answer speed is a function of what exists before the request arrives. A company that closes in 3 days, holds revenue on a consistent cut, and keeps add-back evidence filed as it accrues can absorb three waves of diligence inside three weeks instead of six. That leaves the back half of exclusivity for the purchase agreement, which is where a seller wants his remaining time, because the agreement is where the indemnity caps, the escrow, and the working capital mechanics get decided.

There is a second effect that does not show up on a schedule and matters more. A buyer forms a view of management in the first two weeks of diligence, and he forms it from response time, not from the answers. A seller who returns complete files in days is read as a company under control, and control is priced into how hard the rest of the process gets. A seller who returns partial files in weeks gets a longer list, because the buyer starts checking things he would otherwise have taken on faith. That is the same mechanic behind [a data room that answers the question before it is asked](/perspectives/data-room-that-answers-first).

The cost of building this ahead is real but bounded, and it is paid in a period when the seller still has alternatives. The cost of building it during exclusivity is unbounded, and it is paid in price.
