## At a glance

- Margin by customer usually stops at the invoice. What happens after the invoice, how long the customer takes to pay, is a real cost of serving that customer, and it rarely shows up anywhere the owner looks.
- A slow payer costs the company four ways: cash tied up, interest on the borrowing that covers it, staff time chasing it and a higher chance of never collecting.
- The fix is a short monthly ranking of customers by days to pay, a dollar figure for what the slow ones cost, and one decision per slow account: terms, price, deposit or nothing.

Most owners can name their slowest payer without looking. It's the big account, the one everyone is careful with. It pays, eventually. And because it pays eventually, nobody ever puts a number on what eventually costs.

## Why doesn't the margin report show it?

Because the margin report ends at the invoice. It shows what the company billed the customer and what the work cost, and it treats a customer who pays in 20 days exactly like one who pays in 90. The cost of waiting lands somewhere else: in interest expense on the line of credit, in the bookkeeper's week, in a bad debt line at year end. None of those carry the customer's name.

So the busiest account can look like a good account on every report and still be one of the more expensive customers the company has.

## What does a slow payer actually cost?

Four things, and the first one is the biggest.

1. **The cash tied up.** Every day past terms is money the company has earned and can't use. It isn't paying payroll, buying materials or sitting as a cushion. It's financing the customer.
2. **The interest on the gap.** If the company runs on a line of credit, the slow receivable is being carried on borrowed money. If it doesn't, the cost shows up as decisions not made because the cash wasn't there.
3. **The time spent chasing.** Statements, follow up calls, the owner's own call to the customer's owner. That time has a cost, and it comes out of people who should be doing something else.
4. **The risk of never collecting.** The older a receivable gets, the less of it comes in. A customer who pays in 90 days in a good year is the first to stretch to 150 in a bad one.

Here's an illustration, not a client figure. A customer buys $1.6M a year on 30 day terms and pays, on average, in 80 days. That's 50 extra days, about $219,000 the company has earned and doesn't have. Carried on a line of credit at 8.5%, it costs about $18,600 a year in interest alone, more than a point of margin on that account, before anyone counts the chasing or the write off risk.

## Why does it keep happening?

Because nobody decided to let it. The terms said 30 days. The customer drifted to 45, then 60, then 80, one month at a time, and each month the payment came in, so each month it felt fine. The drift is a decision nobody made.

And the slowest payers are often the biggest customers, so nobody wants to be the one who brings it up. The sales team doesn't want to risk the account. The bookkeeper doesn't have the standing. The owner has a relationship with their owner and would rather not spend it on a collections call.

The human cost is real too. Someone on the team spends the last week of every month on the phone hoping a check arrives before payroll. That's a lot to carry for a customer the reports say is doing fine.

## How do you put a number on it?

Once a month, from the receivables aging and the payment history:

1. **Rank customers by days to pay.** Average days from invoice to cash over the last six months, not the terms on the contract.
2. **Find the gap to terms.** Days to pay minus the terms you agreed. That's the drift.
3. **Dollarize it.** Annual revenue from the customer, times the drift in days, divided by 365. That's the cash tied up. Multiply by your borrowing rate for the carrying cost.
4. **Set it beside the account's margin.** If the account makes 9% after cost to serve and the carrying cost is a point and a quarter, you've lost more than a tenth of its profit to waiting.

The whole book works the same way. Here's another illustration, not a client figure. A $16M company on 30 day terms that collects in 58 days on average has about $1.23M tied up past terms. At 8.5%, that's about $104,000 a year. Bring it to 44 days and roughly $614,000 comes back into the bank.

## What do you do about a slow account?

One decision per account, written down, with a date:

1. **Hold the terms.** Often the cheapest fix. Invoice the day the work is done, send statements on a schedule and make the call at day 35, not day 75. Some customers pay slowly because nobody asks.
2. **Change the terms.** A deposit up front, progress billing on long jobs, or an early payment discount that costs less than the carrying cost it saves.
3. **Price it in.** If the customer won't pay faster and you want the work, the price should carry the cost of waiting. That's a pricing conversation, and the number above is what makes it a fair one.
4. **Leave it.** Sometimes the account is worth the wait. That's a fine answer when it's a decision made with the number in view, not a habit.

Most companies find two or three accounts carry most of the drift. Fixing those is usually enough to change how the month end feels.

## Where this leads

Slow payers are the reason a company can be [profitable and short of cash](/perspectives/profitable-and-short-of-cash) at the same time, and the 13 week view is where the drift shows up first. Days to pay belongs in the same view as [which customer actually makes money](/perspectives/which-customer-makes-money), because the account that looks best on margin can be the one costing the most to carry. And when the answer is to price it in, it's the same work as [pricing off today's cost](/perspectives/price-off-todays-cost).
