## At a glance

- A trailing twelve months is a window, not a total. Every month it gains one month at the front and loses one month at the back, and the buyer prices the net.
- On a $30M manufacturer running $3.6M of trailing EBITDA, a $520,000 record month rolling out and a $300,000 average month rolling in drops trailing EBITDA by $220,000. At 6x that is $1,320,000 of enterprise value, lost on the last day of a month in which nothing about the business changed.

A month you already lived through can cost you $1,320,000 on the day it ages out of your trailing twelve. That is the arithmetic on a $30M-revenue manufacturer priced at 6x trailing EBITDA when a record September rolls out of the window and an ordinary September rolls in. Revenue did not fall. No customer left. The number a buyer underwrites fell by $220,000 because the twelve months he is standing on are not the twelve months he was standing on four weeks ago.

Owners think about the trailing twelve as something that accumulates. Another month closes, another month goes in the file. It does not work that way. The window is fixed at twelve and it moves, so every month is two events: one month arrives and one month leaves. Most of the attention goes to the month arriving, because that is the one still being worked. The month leaving is the one that decides whether the window got better or worse.

## Why does a month leaving matter more than the month arriving?

Because the month arriving is roughly average and the month leaving often is not.

The recent month is the one under management. It reflects the pricing in force, the current cost structure, the current headcount. It usually lands close to the run rate, which is what makes it feel unremarkable. The month leaving is the same calendar month a year earlier, and it carries whatever happened then: the launch that pulled a quarter of orders forward, the insurance settlement, the one week of overtime that never repeated, the customer who bought fourteen months of product in one purchase order.

So the change in the window is the difference between an average month and a historical outlier, and the sign of that difference is set by history rather than by anything happening now. When last year's strongest month is the one rolling off, the trailing twelve falls even in a business that is growing. Owners read that fall as a business problem and go looking for one. There is no business problem. There is a window that moved.

This is also why [a record month deserves an autopsy before it becomes a story](/perspectives/anatomy-of-a-record-month). A record built on a pull-forward is a month that will be in your trailing twelve for exactly twelve months, and then it will be working against you.

## What does the roll-off actually cost?

Put real numbers on it. A manufacturer at $30M of revenue and $3.6M of trailing EBITDA, so $300,000 a month on average. Last September was a record: $520,000 of EBITDA on a large stocking order from the second-largest customer. The order has not repeated and was never expected to.

While the trailing twelve ends in August, the window holds that $520,000. The moment September closes, the window ends in September: the $520,000 comes out, an ordinary $300,000 goes in, and trailing EBITDA is $3,380,000 instead of $3,600,000. At 6x, enterprise value moves from $21,600,000 to $20,280,000. The seller lost $1,320,000 by letting a calendar page turn.

Run it the other way and the same mechanic pays. If the month rolling off is a bad one, say a $140,000 month from a plant shutdown, and an ordinary month replaces it, trailing EBITDA rises $160,000 and enterprise value rises $960,000 with no operating change at all. The window can be a headwind or a tailwind, and which one you are standing in is a matter of public record inside your own general ledger.

The second cost is worse than the first and harder to see. A buyer does not just take the trailing twelve as a level. He reads it as a trend across the last few rolling windows. Three consecutive months in which strong months age out and average months arrive produce a declining trailing twelve, which reads to a diligence team as deceleration. Deceleration is not priced as $220,000. It is priced in the multiple, and a half turn off 6x on $3.4M is another $1,700,000. That is the whole argument for knowing the schedule: the level is a subtraction, and the trend is a re-rate.

## How do you read your own roll-off schedule?

The work is arithmetic and it takes an afternoon once the monthly numbers are true.

1. **Pull monthly EBITDA for 24 months on one consistent basis.** Same accruals, same cutoff, same add-back treatment in every month. A schedule built from months closed under different conventions measures your own inconsistency, not your business. This is the same standard that decides [what counts as a clean trailing twelve](/perspectives/trailing-twelve-what-counts-as-clean).
2. **Mark each month against the 24-month average.** Note the dollar variance, positive or negative. Do not smooth it. The outliers are the entire point of the exercise.
3. **Lay the next 12 months out and write in which month rolls off each one.** December 2026 rolls off December 2025, and so on. That column is your roll-off schedule and it is already fixed.
4. **Assume the arriving months land at run rate, then compute the trailing twelve for each of the next 12 window closes.** Multiply each by your working multiple. You now have enterprise value by launch month, on today's operations.
5. **Name the two or three months where the window is strongest and the ones where it drops hardest.** Those are your launch months and your months to avoid, and the gap between the best and the worst is usually seven figures.

Nothing in that list requires a forecast of the future. It requires an honest history and the willingness to look at it.

## Does this mean waiting for the good window?

No, and this is where the two clocks get confused.

The roll-off schedule tells you which months are cheap to launch in and which are expensive. It does not give you permission to wait, because [every month you wait moves the sale date a month](/perspectives/every-month-moves-the-sale-date), and waiting for a window you cannot substantiate buys nothing. A favorable window is worthless if the months inside it cannot be proven on a consistent basis, because the first thing a diligence team does is rebuild your trailing twelve on its own conventions. A window you cannot defend is not a window.

The order is therefore fixed. Make the months true first. Then read the schedule. Then pick the launch month inside the range the schedule and your own readiness both allow. Owners who run it in the other order, picking a date and then discovering what the window holds, find out during exclusivity that the number they went out on is not the number the buyer arrives at.

There is also a real operating use for this that has nothing to do with selling. An owner who knows a $220,000 headwind is arriving in September can decide in June whether to spend against it, and the decision is a choice rather than a surprise. That is what [counting backward from the sale date](/perspectives/count-backward-from-your-sale-date) is for, applied one month at a time.
