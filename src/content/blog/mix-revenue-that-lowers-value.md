## At a glance

- Not every dollar of revenue is worth the same at a sale. Which lines you grow changes the shape of the whole P&L, and a buyer pays for the shape, not the total.
- Chasing volume through your lowest-margin lines can lift the top line while lowering the multiple, so the company gets bigger and worth less at the same time.
- The owners who build value steer the mix on purpose toward the durable, high-margin lines a buyer rewards, instead of taking whatever revenue is easiest to win.

There's a quiet way to grow a company and make it worth less, and a lot of owners are doing it without knowing: they chase the revenue that's easiest to win, which is usually the revenue worth the least. This piece is about why mix matters more than volume at a sale, and how to steer it deliberately.

## Why does a buyer care about mix?

Because he's buying an earnings stream with a particular shape, and the shape tells him how durable and how profitable the future is. Two companies at $20M in revenue can be worth very different amounts depending on where that revenue comes from: high-margin recurring lines with sticky customers, or low-margin one-off work that has to be re-won every year. The buyer pays a premium for the first shape and a discount for the second, even at identical revenue.

So the total is almost beside the point. A buyer decomposes your revenue into its parts and prices each part on its margin, its durability, and its concentration. Grow the wrong parts and you've handed him more of the revenue he discounts.

## How do owners grow the wrong mix by accident?

Because the easy revenue and the valuable revenue are usually not the same, and the numbers don't flag the difference. The pattern:

1. **The easy line is the low-margin line.** It's easy to win precisely because it competes on price, which is why its margin is thin.
2. **The top line rewards it and the margin doesn't.** Sales and growth targets are usually set on revenue, so the team chases what grows revenue fastest, regardless of what it does to profit.
3. **The average hides the drift.** As the low-margin share of the mix grows, the blended margin softens slowly, and nobody attributes it to mix.
4. **The valuable lines get starved.** Attention and capacity flow to the easy revenue, so the high-margin, durable lines a buyer would pay a premium for never get the push they deserve.

The result is a company that's busier, bigger on paper, and worth less per dollar of revenue than it was before the growth.

## What does steering the mix look like?

It looks like deciding, on purpose, which revenue you want more of and which you'll let go. That means measuring margin and durability by line, setting growth targets on profit and mix rather than revenue alone, and being willing to walk away from easy, low-value work so capacity flows to the lines that actually build worth. For the $15M manufacturer we worked with, part of the move from 15% to 22% margins in 90 days was exactly this: shifting attention and pricing away from the work that looked good on the top line and toward the work that actually paid.

That is value creation through subtraction as much as addition, and it's only possible when you can see the mix clearly enough to choose.
