## At a glance

- Overhead in a growing company rarely jumps. It creeps: a renewal here, a contractor who stayed, a vendor price that went up 6% and nobody pushed back.
- Each cost was a sensible decision the day it started. The total was never a decision at all, and the P&L shows it only as a line that grew a little faster than revenue.
- The fix is to re-decide every recurring cost once: who owns it, what it was meant to buy, and whether you would sign it again today.

Ask an owner what the company spends on software and he will give you a number. Ask him to name every subscription and who uses it, and the room goes quiet. That is the overhead problem in one question. Nothing was wasted on purpose. Every line started as a good idea, approved by someone who needed it, and then it renewed on its own while the business got busy.

## Why does overhead grow faster than anyone decided?

Because costs get decided once and paid forever. A tool is bought for a project. A consultant is brought in for ninety days. A vendor sends a new price with the renewal. Each one is small enough to approve in a minute, and none of them comes back for a second look.

Revenue growth hides it. When sales climb 15%, a cost base that climbs 18% looks like the price of growing. Here is an illustration, not a client figure. A $16M company whose overhead ran 18% of revenue three years ago and runs 21% today is spending $480,000 a year more than the same business needed. Nobody approved $480,000. They approved forty small things that added up to it.

## Where does it hide?

Usually in four places:

1. **Subscriptions.** Software bought by one team, renewed by default, and often duplicated by another team that bought its own. Seats for people who left. Annual plans that auto renew thirty days before anyone looks.
2. **Contractors who became permanent.** Another illustration, not a client figure: a contractor engaged at $8,500 a month for a ninety day project and still billing twenty two months later has cost $187,000. The project may have been worth it. The last year of it was never decided.
3. **Vendor prices that renew up.** Freight, insurance, maintenance contracts and services that carry an increase at every renewal. A few points a year, compounding, with no one assigned to push back.
4. **Overlap across departments.** Two teams paying two vendors for the same service, or two tools that do the same job, because nobody sees the spend by vendor across the whole company.

## Why don't the books show it?

Because the chart of accounts groups spending by type, not by decision. "Software" is one line. "Professional fees" is one line. The report shows the total moved, not which commitment moved it, who owns it, or when it can stop.

So the owner ends up as the cost control. He signs the checks, notices the ones that look large, and lets the rest through because there is no time to chase them. That works until the small ones are most of the problem. It is also a tiring way to run a company: being the only person in the building whose job includes asking "do we still need this?"

## How do you re-decide a cost?

Put every recurring cost on one list and answer four questions for each line:

1. **Who owns it?** One named person who would notice if it stopped. A cost with no owner is the first one to question.
2. **What was it meant to buy?** The project, the problem or the capacity it was supposed to deliver, and whether it still does.
3. **When can it change?** Some costs stop with a phone call. Some need notice by a date in the contract. Some are locked until renewal. The date matters as much as the dollar amount, because a saving you can take in March is worth more than one you can take next year.
4. **Would we sign it today?** Knowing what it costs and what it delivers, at this price, from this vendor. If the answer is no, decide what happens instead: stop it, cut it back, renegotiate it, or put the notice date on the calendar.

This is not a cost cut. A memo telling every team to trim 10% lands on the people doing the profitable work and leaves the forgotten renewals untouched. Re-deciding line by line goes straight to the costs nobody is defending.

## How do you start this week?

You can build a working first version in an afternoon:

1. **Pull twelve months of payments by vendor.** From the bank and the card statements, not the P&L, so nothing hides inside a category.
2. **Keep the ones that recur.** Monthly, quarterly or annual. Sort by annual cost.
3. **Add an owner and a date.** Who uses it, and the next date it can change.
4. **Ask the fourth question on the top twenty.** Most owners find two or three lines where the answer is no. That is usually where the margin went.

The list will be rough. It will still be the first time anyone has seen the overhead as a set of decisions instead of a set of totals.
