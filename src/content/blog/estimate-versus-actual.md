## At a glance

- Most companies that bid their work price it at one margin and finish it at another. The difference is real profit, and the P&L never shows it as a line.
- It leaks in the same few places: labor hours over the estimate, change orders done and never billed, materials bought above the bid price, and work redone at the company's own cost.
- The fix is a short closeout on every finished job: estimate against actual, by line, with the reason for each gap, fed straight back into the next estimate.

Every owner who bids work knows the feeling. The year was busy. The backlog was full. The jobs were priced at the margin the business needs. And the year still finished a few points light, with nobody able to say which jobs took it.

## Why doesn't the P&L show it?

Because the P&L adds every job together. It shows total revenue and total cost of the work for the month, so a job that finished well hides a job that finished badly. The estimate lives in one place, usually the estimating tool or a spreadsheet. The actual cost lives in another, in payroll and the payables. Nobody puts the two side by side once the job is done, because by then the team is on the next one.

Here is an illustration, not a client figure. A $14M company that bids its work at 28% gross margin and finishes the year at 21% has lost seven points somewhere between the estimate and the invoice. That is $980,000 of profit the business priced in and never collected.

## Where does it go?

Usually to four places:

1. **Labor hours.** The bid said 400 hours. The crew spent 520. Nobody decided to spend the extra 120; they were needed to finish, and the job had no hours budget anyone watched while it ran.
2. **Change orders done and not billed.** The customer asked for something extra on site. The team did it to keep the customer happy. The paperwork never caught up, so the work was given away.
3. **Materials above the bid price.** The estimate used last quarter's price. The order went in at this quarter's. On a long job the gap can eat most of the margin on its own.
4. **Rework.** Something done twice, at the company's cost, and charged to the job with no note saying why.

Another illustration, not a client figure. A $500,000 job bid at 25% should leave $125,000. Run 120 extra labor hours at $70 loaded, skip billing $40,000 of change orders, and pay $15,000 more for materials than the bid assumed, and it leaves $61,600. Half the profit on that job went somewhere, and the monthly report shows only a slightly soft month.

## Why does it keep happening?

Because each estimate starts from the last estimate, not from the last actual. If the crew has run 25% over on a certain kind of work for two years, the bid still assumes the original hours, and the company keeps winning the work it loses money on. Low bids win. A bid that is low for the wrong reason wins most of all.

The owner usually senses it. He knows which crews run long and which customers ask for extras. But a sense is hard to take into a pricing conversation, and harder to delegate. He ends up checking every big bid himself, because the numbers that should check it don't exist.

## How do you close a job against its estimate?

Within two weeks of finishing, put one page together for the job with three columns: what the estimate said, what actually happened, and why they differ. Then answer four questions:

1. **Which lines moved?** Labor hours, materials, subcontractors, equipment, and any work outside the original scope.
2. **Was the extra work billed?** Every change done on site should match a change order sent to the customer. The ones that don't are revenue still on the table, if you catch them before the final invoice.
3. **Was the miss in the estimate or in the field?** An estimate that was wrong gets fixed in the estimating standard. A job that ran badly gets fixed in how it is managed. They need different fixes, so name which one it was.
4. **What changes in the next bid?** One adjustment, written into the estimating standard for that kind of work, with a date.

Then look across the closed jobs every quarter. Group them by type of work, by customer and by project manager. The pattern shows up fast: one kind of work that always runs over, one customer whose jobs always grow without the price growing, one crew that finishes under. That is where the margin went, and it is usually concentrated in a few places, not spread evenly across the business.

## How do you start this week?

Take the last ten finished jobs:

1. **Pull the estimate for each one.** The bid as submitted, with its hours and its margin.
2. **Pull the actual cost.** Labor hours from payroll, materials and subcontractors from payables, everything charged to the job.
3. **Put the margin side by side.** Estimated margin, actual margin, and the gap in dollars.
4. **Sort by the gap.** Look at the three biggest misses and write down why each one happened.

Most owners find the same two or three reasons behind most of the gap. Those are the first things to fix in the next estimate.

## Where this leads

The estimate is a price decision made before the work starts, and it drifts the same way [a price set years ago](/perspectives/price-off-todays-cost) drifts. The jobs that run over are often the ones for the customers who [cost the most to serve](/perspectives/which-customer-makes-money), and every big bid is a bet on the [contribution margin behind it](/perspectives/contribution-margin-before-the-big-call).

It matters in dollars. A $15M manufacturer whose largest customer was its least profitable; margins moved from 15% to 22%.
