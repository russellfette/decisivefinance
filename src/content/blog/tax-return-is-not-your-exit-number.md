Most owners can name one earnings figure for their company with confidence, and it is the wrong one. It is the number on the tax return, and on a $20M-revenue company that number can sit $310,000 below what a buyer would underwrite, which is $1,860,000 of price at a 6x multiple. The return is not sloppy. It is accurate, filed, and signed by someone competent. It was simply built to answer a different question than the one a buyer asks, and it arrives nine months after the year it describes.

## Why does the return arrive too late to sell on?

Because the filing calendar and the deal calendar are not the same calendar. A calendar-year company closes 2026 on December 31. The return goes out on the September 15, 2027 extension, roughly nine months later. Between those two dates, the most recent year for which the company has a finished, third-party-touched document is 2025.

So an owner who gets a private-equity call in March 2027 is sitting in the worst spot on the calendar. The buyer wants a trailing twelve months through February 2027. The owner has a filed 2025 return, an unclosed 2026 in his accounting file, and two months of 2027 nobody has looked at. The one document he trusts is describing a year that ended fourteen months before the question.

The trailing-twelve clock doesn't start until the numbers are true, and a filing deadline in September does nothing for a buyer reading in March. That is the whole content of [when the clock actually starts](/perspectives/when-the-clock-actually-starts).

## What does the return get wrong that a buyer cares about?

Nothing, on its own terms. The return answers one question well: what is taxable this year. A buyer asks a different one: what does this company earn, on a normal accrual basis, in a normal year, without the owner in it.

Those two questions pull in opposite directions. A good return defers income and accelerates deductions, because that is what it is for. A buyer's model reverses every one of those choices. Take a $20M company with $2,180,000 of EBITDA as the return supports it:

Two adjustments do most of the work. $205,000 of December work was billed and collected in January, so it lands in the following tax year on the return and in the correct year on accrual. Another $105,000 of prepaid insurance and software was deducted in the year the check cleared rather than spread across the period it covers. Reverse both and the same year earns $2,490,000.

That is a $310,000 swing on the identical twelve months of identical business activity. At 6x it is $1,860,000. It moves in the owner's favor, and he has no document that says so, because the only closed document he owns is the one built to say the opposite.

## Which number does a buyer actually underwrite?

Accrual EBITDA for the trailing twelve months, normalized, with every adjustment supported by something he can tie to a source. Not the return. The return is a corroborating document in diligence, useful for confirming that revenue was reported consistently and that no one has been inventing income. It is never the basis of the price.

This is the distinction between clean books and believable numbers. A return that survived a filing is clean. It is not believable as an earnings statement, because it was not written to be one. [Clean books and believable numbers are two different things](/perspectives/clean-books-vs-believable-numbers), and diligence separates them in the first week.

The cost of not knowing which number you are standing on is that you negotiate from the low one. An owner quoting $2,180,000 has priced his own company at $13,080,000. The defensible figure was $14,940,000. Nobody took $1,860,000 from him. He gave it away, from a document he had every reason to trust.

## What does the sequence look like instead?

The fix is a closing calendar that serves the deal calendar rather than the filing calendar:

1. **Close the year on accrual by the end of January, every year, whether or not anything is happening.** Not a tax close. A decision close, on the basis a buyer prices, finished while the year is still fresh enough to answer questions about.
2. **Build the tax-to-accrual bridge once and keep it current.** Every difference between the return and the accrual view gets a line, a dollar amount, and a source. That schedule is the single most useful thing an owner can hand a buyer's analyst, and almost nobody has one.
3. **Restate the prior two years on the same basis.** A buyer prices a trend, not a year. One good accrual year next to two tax-basis years reads as a company that just started paying attention.
4. **Know your defensible EBITDA before a trigger asks for it.** The number you can support, with the adjustments named and the bridge attached, is the number the conversation starts from.

None of that takes a transaction to justify. It takes one closing cycle, and it survives whether the trigger ever arrives.
