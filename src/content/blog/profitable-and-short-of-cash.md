## At a glance

- Profit and cash are different numbers. A company can grow, post its best year on the P&L, and still be moving money between accounts the week payroll runs.
- The cash goes into places the P&L does not show: customers paying slower, materials bought ahead of an order, loan principal, taxes and owner distributions.
- A 13-week cash forecast, receipts and payments by week, rolled forward every Monday and checked against actuals, shows the squeeze weeks before it lands.

The best year on paper is often the tightest year in the bank. Sales are up, the team is busy, the P&L says the plan is working. Then a large customer pays three weeks late, a materials order comes due, and the owner spends a Thursday night deciding which bills can wait. Nothing about the business is broken. The numbers were built to report profit, and nobody built them to say when that profit turns into cash.

## Why does a profitable company run short of cash?

Because the P&L records a sale when it is earned and a cost when it is incurred. The bank records them when the money moves. In a stable company the two stay close. In a growing one they drift apart, and the gap is the cash squeeze.

Every dollar of growth needs cash before it returns any. You buy the materials, pay the crew and ship the work, then wait for the customer. The faster you grow, the more cash sits in that wait.

## Where does the cash go?

Usually into four places, and the P&L shows none of them clearly:

1. **Customers paying slower.** Here is an illustration, not a client figure. An $18M company whose customers drift from paying in 45 days to 58 days has about $640,000 less in the bank, on the same sales at the same margin. Thirteen days nobody decided on.
2. **Buying ahead of the work.** A large order that needs $400,000 of materials paid 30 days before it ships and collected 60 days after has that cash out for roughly 90 days. On the P&L it is one good month.
3. **Payments below the profit line.** Loan principal, equipment purchases, estimated taxes and owner distributions all leave the bank and never touch the P&L as expenses. A company can earn its profit and spend it twice.
4. **Growth itself.** More revenue means more receivables and usually more inventory, so the balance sheet absorbs cash before the income statement shows a return.

## Why don't the books warn you?

Because they look backward and they arrive late. The month closes two or three weeks after it ends, and the report shows where the cash went, not where it is going. By the time the squeeze shows up in a monthly package, it has already shown up in the bank.

So the owner becomes the forecast. He knows roughly when the big customer pays, roughly what payroll runs, roughly what is due to the supplier. Roughly works until two of those land in the same week. That is a lot to carry, and it usually gets carried at night.

## What is a 13-week cash forecast?

It is a week-by-week list of the cash you expect to receive and the cash you expect to pay, for the next thirteen weeks, starting from today's bank balance. Thirteen weeks is a quarter: far enough out to act, close enough to be accurate.

Three things make it work:

1. **Direct, not derived.** Receipts by customer and payments by vendor, payroll and debt, not a percentage of the P&L.
2. **Rolled forward every week.** Each Monday, drop the week that just ended, add a new week thirteen, and update what changed.
3. **Checked against what happened.** Compare last week's forecast with last week's bank activity. The misses tell you which customers pay late and which costs you keep underestimating. After a month or two, the forecast gets better and the surprises stop.

Set a minimum cash floor and watch the weeks that come near it. A week below the floor six weeks out is a phone call to a customer, a timing conversation with a supplier, or a draw on the line, planned calmly instead of found on payroll Thursday.

## How do you start one this week?

You can build a working first version in a few hours:

1. **Start with today's bank balance.** All operating accounts, one number.
2. **List expected receipts by customer by week.** Use when each customer actually pays, not the invoice terms.
3. **List payments by week.** Payroll on its real dates, rent, the large vendors, debt service, taxes and any planned distributions.
4. **Find the low week.** Then decide what you would do about it now, while there is time.

The first version will be rough. It will still be closer to the truth than a feel for the bank balance, and it gets better every Monday you keep it.
