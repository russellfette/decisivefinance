## At a glance

- The instinct once an owner sees a numbers problem is to dress it up. That instinct is backward. A buyer pays for numbers he can verify, not numbers that look nice.
- Polish a buyer can't verify makes him trust you less, not more, because it reads as advocacy hiding something. Accurate and ugly beats polished and untrustworthy, every time.
- Reframing "get ready to sell" from dressing up the business to making the numbers true changes the whole project, and it's the version that actually raises the price.

There's a reflex that kicks in the moment an owner realizes his numbers won't survive a sale: make them look better. Smooth the edges, build a prettier deck, present the best face. This piece is about why that reflex loses money, and what to do instead.

## Why does polish backfire?

Because a buyer's analyst reads financials for a living, and he's specifically trained to distrust presentation he can't tie to source. When the deck looks better than the data supports, he doesn't relax. He leans in, because in his experience polish is where problems get buried. The nicer the story and the thinner the support, the harder he pulls the thread.

So the effort you spend making the numbers look good actively works against you. It raises the buyer's suspicion at the exact moment you needed his trust, and suspicion gets priced into the offer. You paid to lower your own number.

## What does a buyer actually reward?

Numbers that are true and traceable, even when they're unflattering. A buyer knows the difference between a business with an ugly but honest margin and one with a beautiful but unverifiable one, and he pays more for the first, because he can rely on it. Accurate and ugly gives him something to underwrite. Polished and untrustworthy gives him a reason to discount.

That's the reframe that changes everything about getting ready to sell:

1. **The job isn't presentation. It's truth.** Make each number mean what it says before you worry about how it looks.
2. **Legible beats impressive.** A plain report a stranger can follow beats a slick one he has to take on faith.
3. **Documentation is the polish that works.** The only "polish" a buyer values is a clean trail from claim to source.
4. **Ugly-but-true is a fixable starting point.** An honest low margin tells you where the work is. A fake high one tells you nothing and blows up in diligence.

We don't audit the past and we don't dress it up. We make the numbers true, then make them legible, which is the only version of "ready" a buyer actually pays for.

## What this looks like in practice

The $25M consumer products manufacturer we worked with had every incentive to present his best-looking month, the one near 90% gross margin. It was also fake, the product of a single wrong cost entry. Presenting it would have been polish on a mirage, and a buyer's analyst would have found the entry and discounted everything around it. The work that actually raised his number was the opposite: correcting the entry, telling the true and uglier margin, and documenting it so it could be trusted. Truth, made legible, was worth more than the flattering lie.
