## At a glance

- Every big call in a growing company (a hire, a large contract, a price change, a second location) is a bet on contribution margin: what a dollar of revenue leaves after the costs that move with it.
- Most P&Ls can't show that number. They mix costs that move with revenue and costs that don't, so the owner decides on feel.
- Find it by splitting costs into the ones that rise with each sale and the ones you pay regardless, then run every big call through it before you commit.

The next big call on your desk probably sounds like a people question or a growth question. Should we hire two more project managers? Should we take the contract that doubles our volume with one customer? Should we raise prices and risk a few accounts? Underneath each one sits the same number, and most owners of growing companies don't have it in front of them when they decide.

## What is contribution margin?

Contribution margin is what each dollar of revenue leaves behind after the costs that move with that dollar. Materials, direct labor you add when work comes in, freight, sales commissions, card fees: those rise and fall with volume. Rent, the controller, the software stack, the leadership team: those you pay whether you sell a dollar or ten million of them.

What's left after the moving costs is the money available to cover the fixed ones and then become profit. That is the contribution. Expressed as a share of revenue, it is the contribution margin.

Gross margin on most P&Ls is not the same thing. It usually mixes some fixed costs into cost of sales and leaves some variable costs down in operating expenses, because the chart of accounts was set up to file the tax return.

## Why does it matter for the big call?

Because a fixed cost has to be paid for out of contribution, and the contribution margin tells you how much new revenue that takes.

Here is an illustration, not a client figure. A hire costs $120,000 a year fully loaded. At a 25% contribution margin, the company needs $480,000 of new revenue before that hire pays for itself. At a 40% contribution margin, it needs $300,000. Same person, same salary, a $180,000 difference in what the hire has to bring in.

The same math runs every other call:

1. **The big contract.** A customer that doubles volume at a lower price can shrink contribution per dollar enough that the company works harder and keeps less. A $15M manufacturer whose largest customer was its least profitable; margins moved from 15% to 22%.
2. **The price change.** A 5% price increase on a 25% contribution margin business lifts contribution per dollar by a fifth. You can lose a meaningful share of volume before the increase stops paying. That is an illustration too, and it is why the number has to be yours, not a rule of thumb.
3. **The second location or the new line.** It carries its own fixed costs, so the question is how many months of its contribution it takes to cover them, and whether you have the cash to wait.

## Why don't most owners have the number?

Because the numbers answer what happened, not what to do. Contribution margin is the number for what to do. The bookkeeper codes a cost where the tax return wants it. The monthly close produces a P&L that is right in total and silent on which costs move with volume. Nobody producing the numbers is paid to split them for the next decision, so nobody does.

So the owner carries it in his head. He knows roughly what a job costs, roughly what the team can absorb, roughly what a price increase would do. Roughly is fine until the call is large enough that being wrong by ten points of margin is a year of profit.

## How do you find yours?

You can get a working number in an afternoon with last year's P&L and a pencil:

1. **List every cost line and mark it moving or fixed.** Ask one question per line: if revenue dropped 20% next month, would this cost drop with it? Some lines are mixed; split them by your best estimate and note it.
2. **Add up the moving costs and divide by revenue.** One minus that share is your contribution margin.
3. **Run it by customer or job if you can.** The company number is an average. The big calls usually turn on one customer, one product line or one kind of job, and that is where the average hides the answer.
4. **Write down the big call and the revenue it needs to pay for itself.** Then check that number against what you actually expect, in writing, before you commit.

The first pass will be imperfect. It will still be closer to the truth than the feel you would otherwise decide on, and it gets better every month you keep it current.
