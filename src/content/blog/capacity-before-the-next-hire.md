## At a glance

- Most growth plans add people in proportion to revenue: grow 15%, grow the team 15%. Payroll doesn't work that way. It moves in steps.
- Some teams can carry a lot more revenue with the people they have. Others are already at the wall. A plan that treats them the same hires too early in one place and too late in the other.
- The fix is a capacity check by team before the plan is locked: how much more each team can carry, what triggers the next seat, and a date for each hire tied to that trigger instead of to January.

This is the week a lot of owners finish the 2027 plan. The revenue line goes up, and the headcount line goes up with it, usually at about the same slope. It looks reasonable. It's also where a good share of next year's profit gets committed before anyone checks whether the seats are needed.

## Why doesn't headcount grow with revenue?

Because people come in whole units and work isn't spread evenly. A sales team of four might be running at 70% of what it can carry. A warehouse crew might be at 95%. A customer service team might have room on paper and none in practice, because one large account eats half its week.

So the ratio the plan uses (revenue per employee, payroll as a percent of revenue) is an average of teams in very different places. Averages are fine for describing last year. They're a poor way to decide next year's seats.

There's a second reason the straight line misleads. When a team has room, the next dollar of revenue it brings in carries almost no added payroll. That makes growth through that team far more profitable than the company's overall margin suggests. When a team is at the wall, the next dollar carries a whole salary with it, and that growth is less profitable than the plan assumes.

## What does hiring on the straight line cost?

Here's an illustration, not a client figure. A $22M distributor plans 2027 at $25M and budgets five new seats at $95,000 each, loaded, because headcount has always run about in step with revenue.

A capacity check by team tells a different story. Sales can carry the extra $3M with the people it has; two of the reps are well under their book. Customer service has room once one account's requests are routed properly. The warehouse is at its limit and needs one more person by spring. Purchasing needs one when volume crosses a level the plan reaches in the third quarter.

Two hires instead of five. That's $285,000 of payroll the company doesn't carry, on a plan that still hits $25M.

And it runs the other way. If the warehouse hire waits until the backlog shows up, the cost is overtime, late shipments and a team running tired into the busiest months. Hiring too late costs money too. It just shows up somewhere other than the payroll line.

## Why does it keep happening?

Because the plan gets built from the top. Revenue first, then a payroll percentage that looks like last year's, then the headcount that fills it. Nobody asks each team leader how much more the team can do before it breaks, and the leaders who are asked tend to say they need help, because nobody ever got in trouble for asking.

The human side matters here too. A hire made too early is hard on the person you hired. They join to find there isn't enough work, and they're the first one people look at when the year gets tight. A hire made too late burns out the people already doing the work, often the ones you most want to keep.

## How do you check capacity before you commit?

For each team, before the plan locks:

1. **Name what the team's work is driven by.** Orders shipped, accounts served, jobs run, invoices processed. One driver per team, the thing that actually creates the work.
2. **Measure what the team handles today.** The driver volume over the last six months, per person. Use what happened, not what the job description says.
3. **Find the ceiling.** Ask the team lead, then check it against the best sustained month on record. The ceiling is the volume the team can hold without overtime becoming normal.
4. **Run next year's plan through it.** The plan's driver volume against the ceiling, month by month. The month it crosses is the month the next seat needs to start, less however long it takes to hire and train.
5. **Write the trigger, not the date.** "We add the warehouse seat when we're shipping 1,400 orders a week for three weeks running" holds up when the plan moves. "We add the warehouse seat in March" doesn't.

Then cost the seats that survive, and look at what's left. Where a team has room, new revenue through that team is worth more than the overall margin says, and that's useful to know when a big contract or a price decision comes up.

## What does this change in the plan?

Usually three things:

1. **Fewer seats up front.** The hires that survive the check are the ones the work actually needs. The rest move to a trigger.
2. **Better timing on the ones you need.** The seats at the wall get hired before the wall, not after the overtime bill.
3. **A sharper price.** If growth through one team needs a whole new seat, that cost belongs in the price of the work that drives it. If it doesn't, you have room to compete for it.

Most companies find one or two teams with far more room than the averages show, and one team quietly at the limit that nobody planned for.
