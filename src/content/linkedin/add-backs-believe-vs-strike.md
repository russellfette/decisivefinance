An owner handed a buyer twelve add-backs. He struck seven, then stopped believing the other five.

That's the part nobody warns you about.

An add-back is a cost you pull out of historical earnings because it won't follow the business to a new owner. The above-market owner salary. The lawsuit you settled once. The truck that isn't really a company truck.

Done right it's fair, and the math is big. A buyer prices off a multiple of earnings, so a defensible $100,000 add-back on a 5x business is worth $500,000 at the table.

So owners pad the list. It feels like free money.

Here's what I watch happen instead. The "one-time" expense that shows up three years running gets struck. So does the owner comp add-back with no replacement cost behind it. And once a buyer catches you reaching, he reads the whole schedule as advocacy instead of fact, and discounts the good ones with the junk.

A clean schedule of five can beat a padded schedule of twelve. He pays his multiple on the number he believes, not the number you claim.

So the schedule isn't really a negotiation. It's a credibility test you take before the negotiation starts.

The fix isn't clever. Build the short, defensible version and document it now, before anyone asks. Accurate and ugly beats polished and untrustworthy.
