If every price, every hire, and every big customer runs through you, a buyer isn't buying a company. He's buying your job, and he pays job money.

Owner-dependence is the discount I see owners least ready for, because it never shows up on a P&L. There's no line item. It leaves fingerprints.

The top accounts are "yours," so a buyer sees them walking out the door with you.

Pricing lives in your head, so the margin leaves when you do.

Your team executes well but still routes every real call to you. The decision layer is one person deep.

Here's the math on a company earning $3 million. Every turn of multiple a buyer takes off for that risk is $3 million off the price. Whatever he does pay tends to come back as earnouts and holdbacks, built to keep you around long enough to download what you know.

The hard part: it's fixable, but not in a quarter. A buyer can tell real transfer from a reorg done last spring. The owners who get paid for independence spend years making themselves less necessary, and the trend shows.

And the human side: the same dependence that caps the price is usually why a week away from the business feels impossible.

If a sale is anywhere in your next three to five years, this is the one to start on first. Does that match what you're seeing? Full piece linked in the comments.
