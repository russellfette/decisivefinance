Signing the LOI feels like the finish line. It's the moment you hand over your calendar.

Here's what I mean. Exclusivity usually runs 60 to 90 days. You stop talking to anyone else while the buyer finishes diligence. He gets time without competition, you get a price on paper.

That's a fine trade if you can keep pace. But diligence requests arrive in waves, and each wave gets written off the answers to the last one. Take two weeks per round and you've spent six weeks of a ten-week window on three exchanges, with the purchase agreement still undrafted.

Then the window closes and the buyer offers an extension. Extensions get priced. On a $24M enterprise value, a 5% concession is $1,200,000, agreed to in an afternoon by an owner who already told his team the company is sold.

Here's the thing. None of the questions eating that clock are hard. They're just unassembled. Revenue by customer by month on one consistent cut. Add-back support with a document behind each one. Monthly working capital detail. The answers are in your company, just never in one place.

That's why close speed matters more than owners think. A 22-day close means your most recent month is unavailable three weeks out of four. I've taken a close from 22 days to 3, and the same request goes from a two-week scramble to a same-week answer.

You can't take the clock back after you sign it away. You can build the answer file before.

If you're inside two years of a sale, does your team answer in days or in weeks?
