Your biggest customer is the reason you're proud of the company. To a buyer, it's the fastest way his purchase disappears.

Say one account is 40% of revenue. To you, that's proof of trust.

To him, one relationship, probably yours, controls almost half of what he's paying for. If it walks in year one, he bought a smaller business. So he prices it.

Here's the part that stings. Concentration hits your multiple, not your earnings.

Two companies, same $2 million of profit. One earns it from thirty customers, the other from three. The diversified book gets the full multiple. The concentrated one gets a discount on every dollar of earnings, and the price can land at half.

What a buyer weighs:

1. Share of revenue in your top one, three, and five accounts.
2. Whose relationship it is, yours or the company's.
3. How easily that customer could switch tomorrow.
4. Contracts and switching costs, or a handshake.

Every one of those can be improved before a sale.

So the move that feels backward is often right. Capping the anchor account, growing the middle of the book, and moving relationships off you can lower revenue a little while raising what the company is worth.

You can only manage this once you can see profit by customer, and most owners can't.
