There was about $57,600 of value on this company's balance sheet that did not exist.

Not stolen. Not fraud. Just never checked.

A $25M consumer products manufacturer, getting the numbers ready for a possible sale. Two lines told the story.

Inventory carried roughly $22,600 higher than what was actually in the warehouse. Dead stock that never got written off, because writing it down felt like admitting a loss.

A prepaid supply line overstated by about $35,000, because closing the month never required anyone to draw it down against what got used.

Here is why that matters more than it sounds. An overstated asset lies twice. It inflates the equity a buyer thinks he is paying for, and it bends the margin the owner reads to run the business.

Then a buyer's analyst counts the warehouse. He finds value that is not there, writes it down, and starts asking what else on the balance sheet was never verified. One stale line becomes doubt priced across the whole deal.

We tied it out and rebuilt the close so the balance sheet came in verified instead of assumed. The $57,600 came off. That sounds like a loss. It is the opposite. It replaced a number the owner could not defend with one he could.

Most owners audit their P&L and trust their balance sheet. A buyer does the reverse.

When did someone last tie every asset on your books to something real, a count, a contract, a statement?

If you are a year or two from selling, that is worth knowing before a buyer knows it for you.
