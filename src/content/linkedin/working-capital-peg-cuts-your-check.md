A buyer agreed to the price, then cut the owner's check by $450,000 at close. On a term he never negotiated.

It is called the working capital peg, and it is where clean-looking deals quietly leak cash.

Here is how it works.

The price is quoted cash-free, debt-free. The business has to be delivered with a "normal" level of working capital. The buyer defines normal as a number, usually the trailing-twelve-month average.

At close, the actual working capital is trued up against that peg. Below it, and the price drops dollar for dollar.

Now the trap. That trailing average is built on the same books that were never built to decide from. Aged receivables, dead inventory, early payments. So the target gets set high.

Then the owner does what feels smart before a sale. Pushes collections, thins the warehouse, stretches vendors. Working capital drops to $3.15M against a $3.6M peg.

He improved the business and paid $450,000 for it at the closing table.

The fix is not clever. Rebuild the working capital number a buyer will use before he sets it, on books that tell the truth. Then model the true-up in dollars before you sign.
