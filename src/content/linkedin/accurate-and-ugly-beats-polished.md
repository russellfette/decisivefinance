The best-looking month in a $25M manufacturer's P&L was the one that would have cost him the most.

Gross margin near 90%. Every incentive said lead with it.

It was also fake. One wrong cost entry, and the whole month was a mirage.

Here's the reflex I see the moment an owner realizes his numbers won't survive a sale: make them look better. Smoother edges, prettier deck, best face forward.

That reflex loses money.

A buyer's analyst reads financials for a living, and he's trained to distrust anything he can't tie to source. When the deck looks better than the data under it, he doesn't relax. He leans in. In his experience, polish is where problems get buried.

So the effort you spend dressing up the numbers raises his suspicion at the exact moment you needed his trust, and suspicion gets priced into the offer. You paid to lower your own number.

What he rewards is the opposite. A true, uglier margin with a clean trail from claim to source gives him something to underwrite, and it tells you where the work is.

For that manufacturer, the work that raised his number was correcting the entry, telling the real margin, and documenting it so a stranger could follow it.

Accurate and ugly beats polished and untrustworthy. Buyers know the difference.
